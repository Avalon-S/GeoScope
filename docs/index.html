Read the abstract

In ultra-high-resolution (UHR) remote sensing images, the objects that questions count in a region or relate by direction often span only a few pixels. Existing methods mainly decide where a vision–language model (VLM) should look and trust its answer in the chosen view. We show that this assumption fails for counting, and measure VLMs as one calibrates a sensor. All eight open VLMs tested fall increasingly short once a view holds more than a certain number of objects, about five for most on DOTA aerial images. This counting horizon narrows as views grow and objects appear smaller. An empirical power-law response model predicts the undercount a split recovers and the spurious counts it adds, and supports the rule of splitting only views beyond the horizon. In GeoScope, a text-only language model parses the question into a typed spatial program that fixes the region and its resolution. The VLM only grounds and counts within its operating range, and directions are computed explicitly from the grounded boxes. To our knowledge, it is the first UHR method to split views by a measured limit of the VLM. Without training, it improves Qwen3-VL-8B by 16.3 points on regional counting and 10.5 on spatial relations in XLRS-Bench, and has the highest reported accuracy, trained methods included, on regional counting and on the whole benchmark by question. On UHR-Micro it improves global and regional counting by 16.8 and 36.2 points, 15.6 of the former from splitting. On MME-RealWorld counting it gains 11.4 points, and we show that an option that is almost never correct yet often chosen distorts comparisons there.

The finding

A VLM counts reliably up to about five objects and estimates beyond.

We cut 960 crops from DOTA-v1.5 training images, none of them in any benchmark we evaluate, and asked Qwen3-VL-8B how many cars, trucks, ships or storage tanks each one holds. Every dot is one crop. Up to about five objects the answers scatter around the truth. Beyond that most fall below it, and many are round numbers. The shortfall also depends on the view. Crops of 256 pixels, shown enlarged threefold, stay within 21% up to 160 objects, while crops of 2,048 pixels at native resolution fall short at every number of objects. Switch to subdivision to see the same crops counted by GeoScope's rule.

GeoScope

The question becomes a typed program, and the executor chooses every view.

A frozen Qwen3-4B reads only the question and writes a typed program. It never sees the image or the options. A deterministic executor runs the program and uses the frozen VLM through two narrow ports only: ground a phrase in a window, or count a phrase in a window. The executor decides which windows, at what scale, and how the answers combine. Pick an example and click a stage.

Counting below the horizon

Stitch every view into parts until each holds five objects or fewer.

The executor asks the VLM to count the whole region first. A count above five is an estimate, so the view is split into four and each part is counted again, down to cells of 256 pixels. A zero from a view shown at reduced resolution is not trusted either, because small objects vanish when a view is shrunk. The answer is the sum over the accepted views. Step through it.

Simulated VLM with the behaviour measured above: exact within the horizon, a round-number undercount beyond it, and blind to objects smaller than about four pixels in the view it is shown. The real model is not quite exact within the horizon.

Hierarchical referring

Find the place first, then look inside it.

Small objects are usually named through the place that contains them. On a 10,000-pixel image shown whole, the place is visible and the object is not. GeoScope grounds the place, then searches for the object inside the place's window, which the same pixel budget shows at close to native resolution.

“Where is the grey tennis court in the residential area right of centre relative to the sports field?”
A real failure from our development split, redrawn. On the OSR development questions, grounding the place first raises accuracy from 49 to 56%.
Geometry from boxes

Directions computed from the extents of the two boxes.

The angle between two box centres misreads large objects. GeoScope tiles the plane by the reference object's extent, like the panes of a window lattice, and averages the tiles the target covers, as the direction-relation matrix of spatial databases does. Drag either box.

On OSR the matrix adds 10 development and 2.0 test points over the angle between box centres.

Results

The same frozen VLM, 10 to 16 points better on held-out tests.

All design decisions were made on a 20% development split. Each frozen version then ran once on the remaining 80%. Bars show test accuracy with 95% bootstrap intervals. Every reproduced method ran its official code on the same test questions, and its gain is measured against its own zero-shot backbone: Qwen3-VL-8B for WeaveEarth, GazeEarth and RAP, LLaVA-OV-7B for ZoomSearch.

XLRS-Bench, published results

All questions unless noted. Average: over the 13 subtasks; by question: over all 3,080. Programs answer RC, OSR and overall counting, close-ups answer the questions that give a pixel box, and the backbone the rest.

MME-RealWorld remote sensing

All questions unless noted. GeoScope answers position and colour with its backbone's zero-shot answers.

UHR-Micro

Test questions, official metric: a count earns partial credit by its relative error. Global, regional and conditional counting; direction and distance between two marked objects.

Seconds per question

MME-RS counting on one RTX 3090. GeoScope and zero-shot answering are timed from scratch on development questions, synthesis included; the reproduced methods on the test questions. ZoomSearch and RAP include swapping their two models in and out of memory.
